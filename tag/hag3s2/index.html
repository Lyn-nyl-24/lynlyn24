Vector spaces and lines

Section 3.2 · Projective geometry

Our goal is to define the set ℙn(R)\mathbb{P}^{n}(R) for an arbitrary ring R.R. To that end, we need to make sense of lines over R.R. In this section, we define vector spaces and lines over rings, as well as subspaces and quotient spaces.

Remark 3.6 · Modules of constant rank 0#

By Propositions 3.1(iv) and 2.77(i), a vector space is zero if and only if it has constant rank 0. Note that this is not true for more general modules. For example, the ℤ\mathbb{Z}-module ℚ/ℤ\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z} has constant rank 0.

Definition 3.8 · Line#

An RR-module satisfying the equivalent conditions of Proposition 3.7 is called a line over R.R. We denote by Line Vect the full subcategory of lines. A line is called trivial if it is isomorphic to R.R.

Definition 3.9 · Universally injective map#

Let RR be a ring and let MM and NN be RR-modules. An RR-linear map f:M→Nf:M\rightarrow N is called universally injective if, for every ring map R→S,R\rightarrow S, the map f⊗RS:M⊗RS→f\otimes _{R}S:M\otimes _{R}S\rightarrow N⊗RSN\otimes _{R}S is injective.

Remark 3.10#

(i) If the RR-linear map f:M→Nf:M\rightarrow N is universally injective, then for every RR-module P,f⊗RP:M⊗RP,f\otimes _{R}P:M\otimes _{R} P→N⊗RPP\rightarrow N\otimes _{R}P is injective. This follows from applying the definition of universal injectivity to the ring map R→R\rightarrow Sym and using that PP is a direct summand of Sym

(ii) We need not consider the dual notion of universally surjective map, since surjective maps are preserved by base change. In other words, every surjective map is universally surjective.

Example 3.11#

(i) Any map with a retraction (equivalently, any direct summand inclusion) is universally injective.

(ii) The inclusion is an example of a universally injective map of abelian groups that does not admit a retraction.

(iii) Let f∈R.f\in R. The “multiplication by f”f\text{”} map R→RR\rightarrow R is injective if and only if ff is not a zero divisor, but it is universally injective if and only if ff is a unit (as it becomes the zero map after base change to R/f).R/f).

Remark 3.12#

By Proposition 2.77, “vector space” and “line” are Zariski-local properties of modules, and “universally injective” is a Zariski-local property of linear maps. In fact, by Proposition 3.1(iv), “vector space” and “line” are precisely the minimal weakenings of the properties “free of finite rank” and “free of rank 1” that are Zariski-local.

Definition 3.13 · Subspace and quotient space#

Let VV be an RR-module.

(i) A subspace of VV is a submodule U⊂VU\subset V such that UU is a vector space and the inclusion map is universally injective.

(ii) A quotient space of VV is a quotient module V↠WV\twoheadrightarrow W such that WW is a vector space.

Remark 3.19#

In practice, we do not distinguish between submodules of MM and monomorphisms into M,M, nor between quotient modules of MM and epimorphisms out of M:M: we may always identify a monomorphism with its image and an epimorphism with its coimage. Two monomorphisms into MM have the same image if and only if they are isomorphic over MM (in which case the isomorphism is unique). Similarly, two epimorphisms out of MM have the same coimage if and only if they are (uniquely) isomorphic under M.M. These identifications are happening for example in the statement of Corollary 3.18.

Warning 3.20#

Let f:V→Wf:V\rightarrow W be a map between vector spaces. In general, neither ker(f)f) nor coker(f)f) is a vector space. By Propositions 3.14 and 3.15, ker(f)f) is a vector space if ff is surjective, and coker(f)f) is a vector space if ff is universally injective (but injectivity does not suffice).
