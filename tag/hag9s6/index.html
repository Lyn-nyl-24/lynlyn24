Section 9.6 · Smoothness

Recall that a scheme XX is discrete if its underlying space ∣X∣\vert X\vert is discrete, and that the fibers of a morphism of schemes Y→XY\rightarrow X are the schemes Yx=Y×XY_{x}=Y\times _{X} Spec(κ(x))\kappa (x)) for x∈∣X∣.x\in \vert X\vert .

Definition 9.100 · Locally quasi-finite, quasi-finite morphism#

A morphism of schemes is locally quasi-finite if it is locally of finite type and its fibers are discrete. It is quasi-finite if it is locally quasi-finite and quasi-compact.

Remark 9.101#

“Locally quasi-finite” is a local property of morphisms of schemes.

Remark 9.102 · Fiberwise finiteness#

A map is quasi-finite if and only if it is of finite type and has finite fibers. Indeed, the following conditions are equivalent for a scheme XX of finite type over a field k:k:

(i) XX is discrete;

(ii) XX has Krull dimension ≤0;\leq 0;

(iii) XX has finitely many points;

(iv) XX is finite over kk (Definition 3.91). The implication (i) ⇒\Rightarrow (iii) holds whenever XX is quasi-compact, and the implication (ii) ⇒\Rightarrow (i) holds whenever XX is locally noetherian. The implication (iv) ⇒\Rightarrow (ii) follows from Theorem 8.58(ii). The implication (iii) ⇒\Rightarrow (ii) follows from Theorem 8.65: the generic points of the irreducible components are open, so that their residue fields are of finite type over kk and hence of transcendence degree 0. Finally,

(ii) and (iii) imply that X≃X\simeq Spec(R)R) where RR is a noetherian kk-algebra of Krull dimension ≤0,\leq 0, hence artinian. Thus, RR admits a finite filtration by ideals whose successive quotients are isomorphic to residue fields of R.R. Since these fields are themselves finite extensions of k,k, this proves (iv).

Example 9.103#

(i) By Corollary 9.67, unramified morphisms of schemes are locally quasi-finite.

(ii) By Remark 9.102, finite morphisms of schemes are quasi-finite.

Remark 9.109 · The étale topology#

By Corollary 9.7, the general recipe of Example 6.29 applies to the classes of smooth, unramified, and étale maps of rings. In particular, there is a pretopology on CAlg whose covers are finite families of étale maps (R→Ri)i∈I(R\rightarrow R_{i})_{i\in I} such that Spec(Ri)→R_{i})\rightarrow Spec(R)R) is surjective. The associated Grothendieck topology on CAlg is called the étale topology. Like the Zariski topology, the étale topology extends to the category of schemes and even to the whole category of algebraic functors (see Example 6.37). The topology defined by the larger class of smooth maps turns out to coincide with the étale topology. Since open immersions are étale, the étale topology is finer than the Zariski topology, and since étale maps are flat, it is coarser than the fppf topology:

An étale sheaf on a scheme XX is a sheaf on the site (Ét ét), where Ét Sch is the full subcategory of étale XX-schemes. This notion was the original motivation for the formalism of Grothendieck topologies and sheaves, as the category of étale sheaves on a scheme is usually not equivalent to any category of sheaves on a topological space. The étale topology plays a very important role in algebraic geometry, as the Zariski topology is too coarse for some purposes, such as having a useful cohomology theory with constant coefficients. Indeed, for any irreducible scheme, constant sheaves of abelian groups have trivial higher Zariski cohomology, but their étale cohomology is interesting. A famous theorem of Grothendieck states that the étale cohomology of any quasi-projective ℂ\mathbb{C}-scheme X⊂ℙℂnX\subset \mathbb{P}^{n}_{\mathbb{C}} with coefficients in any finite abelian group is isomorphic to the singular cohomology of X(ℂ)⊂ℙn(ℂ).X(\mathbb{C})\subset \mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{C}). But the former makes sense for any scheme and so can be used as a replacement for singular cohomology. For example, from the perspective of the étale topology, projective spaces and elliptic curves over any separably closed field look like complex projective spaces and tori. Fields that are not separably closed themselves have a nontrivial étale site: for example, it turns out that Spec(𝔽q)\mathbb{F}_{q}) has the same étale cohomology as a topological circle. Another advantage of the étale topology is that it allows us to compute certain quotients. Consider for example a finite group GG acting freely on a scheme X.X. Then the quotients a and a are usually different. Under mild technical assumptions (which hold if XX is quasi-projective over some ring), a is a scheme and is in fact the quotient of XX by GG in the category of schemes.
