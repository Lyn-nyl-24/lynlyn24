Remark 2.27#

In summary, given a system of polynomial equations Σ over k,k, we have the following relations between Σ and Sol

(i) The data of the pullback square (2.19) is equivalent to the data of Σ itself.

(ii) The data of the embedding Sol is equivalent to the data of the ideal (Σ) in the polynomial ring

(iii) The data of the affine kk-scheme Sol alone is equivalent to the data of the kk-algebra k[Σ].k[\Sigma ]. This can be compared with the following types of data in differential geometry:

(i) A smooth manifold MM given as the vanishing locus of a C-map ℝn→ℝm.\mathbb{R}^{n}\to \mathbb{R}^{m}.

(ii) A smooth manifold MM given as a closed submanifold of ℝn.\mathbb{R}^{n}.

(iii) A smooth manifold M.M. Smooth manifolds are the basic objects of interest in differential geometry. Embedding a manifold MM into a Euclidean space or realizing it as the vanishing locus of a function are often useful ways to understand M,M, but we do not consider this additional data to be part of the manifold MM itself. The situation in algebraic geometry is entirely similar: the basic objects of interest are affine schemes. Any affine scheme XX can be embedded into an affine space or realized as the solution functor of a system of polynomial equations (X≃(X\simeq Sol but this data is not part of the affine scheme XX itself.

A key difference between differential geometry and algebraic geometry is that it is much easier to embed smooth manifolds into ℝn\mathbb{R}^{n} than it is to embed schemes into 𝔸n.\mathbb{A}^{n}. In fact, the former is always possible under mild technical assumptions (which are usually taken as part of the definition of smooth manifold), but many interesting schemes are not affine. For example, the real projective space ℙn(ℝ)\mathbb{P}^{n}(\mathbb{R}) can be embedded in the Euclidean space ℝ2n,\mathbb{R}^{2n}, but we will see that the algebraic projective space ℙn\mathbb{P}^{n} with n≥1n\ge 1 cannot be embedded in 𝔸N\mathbb{A}^{N} for any N.N.
