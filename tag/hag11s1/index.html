Prim and proper morphisms

Section 11.1 · Properness

Properness is the most fundamental global property in geometry: it is essentially about the existence and uniqueness of limits, in the sense of analysis. In classical algebraic geometry, it is called “completeness”, although that name has a different (but closely related) meaning in differential geometry. We start by reviewing the notion of properness in topology. Let XX and YY be topological spaces. A continuous map f:Y→Xf:Y\to X is called universally closed if, for every continuous map Z→X,Z\to X, the base change Y×XZ→ZY\times _{X}Z\to Z is a closed map. By the “tube lemma”, ff is universally closed if and only if it is closed and all its fibers are quasi-compact. Thus, the unique map X→∗X\to \ast is universally closed if and only if XX is quasi-compact. The map ff is called separated if its diagonal is closed (or equivalently universally closed, since it is injective). The unique map X→∗X\to \ast is separated if and only if XX is Hausdorff. Finally, the map ff is called proper if it is both universally closed and separated. Universal closedness has a standard characterization in terms of lifting of limit points. Namely, f:Y→f:Y\to XX is universally closed if and only if, for every generalized sequence s:I→Ys:I\to Y and any limit point xx of there exists a subsequence of ss with a limit point yy such that f(y)=x.f(y)=x. Note that this is almost a right lifting property with respect to (Definition 9.82), except that we may need to pass to a subsequence; this is because the point ∞\infty in I∪{∞}I\cup \{\infty \} can be approached from II is more than one way (namely, via any subsequence). It turns out that universal closedness can be characterized by a right lifting property with respect to certain inclusion maps such that “every point in VV can be approached from in one and only one way”. The simplest example is the inclusion of the generic point of the Sierpiński space: another one is the inclusion map Once universal closedness is characterized by a right lifting property, separatedness and properness are in turn characterized by the “at most one” and “exactly one” versions of the same lifting property (by Remark 9.83(ii,iii)). In algebraic geometry, we have already defined “universally closed” (Definition 5.32) and “separated” (Definition 7.25) for maps of algebraic functors. Analogously to the topological situation recalled above, we will show in §11.2 that for maps of schemes these classes can be characterized by right lifting properties, called the valuative criteria for universal closedness and separatedness. The actual definition of properness will also involve a finiteness condition: a morphism of schemes will be called prim if it is universally closed and locally of finite type, and proper if it is both prim and separated. Thus, prim, separated, and proper morphisms will be characterized by the combination of a right lifting property and a finiteness property, similarly to smooth, unramified, and étale morphisms (Corollary 9.97). Definition 11.1 · Prim, separated, and proper morphisms#

Let f:Y→Xf:Y\to X be a morphism of schemes.

(i) ff is called prim if it is locally of finite type and universally closed (Definition 5.32).

(ii) ff is called separated if its diagonal is prim.

(iii) ff is called proper if it is prim and separated.

Remark 11.2#

(i) The diagonal of a morphism of schemes is always an immersion (Proposition 7.26), and immersions are locally of finite type (Example 2.87). Moreover, closed immersions are universally closed (Propositions 5.28 and 2.69(ii)). Hence, Definition 11.1(ii) is consistent with Definition 7.25.

(ii) Proper morphisms are often defined with the additional condition of being quasi-compact, but this is in fact a consequence of universal closedness (see Theorem 11.21). Hence, prim and proper morphisms are of finite type.

The properties “prim”, “separated”, and “proper” are local on the base and stable under composition and base change.

Remark 11.4#

One can show that these properties are even fpqc-local on the base, but none of them are local on the source: an open immersion that is not closed is not prim, and any morphism whose source is affine is separated (Proposition 7.27(i)).

Example 11.5 · Closed immersions#

As we noted in Remark 11.2(i), closed immersions are prim. They are also separated by Example 7.29(i), hence proper. Conversely, if is a prim immersion and if we factor it as a closed immersion followed by an open immersion then UU and the complement of the image of form an open covering of XX where ii is a closed immersion, so that ii is a closed immersion. In fact, one can show that prim monomorphisms are closed immersions, but this is more difficult (cf. Example 9.10).

Example 11.6 · Integral and finite morphisms#

By the going-up theorem (Theorem 8.58(i)), integral maps of algebraic functors are universally closed; they are also separated, since they are affine. Since “finite” is equivalent to “integral and locally of finite type”, finite maps of schemes are proper.
